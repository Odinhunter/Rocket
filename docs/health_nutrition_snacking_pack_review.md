# The audience world — for review

**What this is:** the file that tells the simulated buyers what the real world looks like. Which brands exist, what things cost, where people shop, what's in the air. They are only allowed to know what's in here.

Built 14 August 2026 from six research sweeps. Every number below carries a source. Where we couldn't source something, it's listed as an open question rather than filled in with something plausible.

**Please read it as a brand person, not as a technical reviewer.** The question is: *does this describe the market you actually sell in?* Anything you disagree with is more useful to us than anything you nod at.

---

## What changed, and why

The last panel judged a protein wafer bar whose whole pitch was "better than your 4pm chocolate bar." The buyers doing the judging lived in a world of 20 brands, all protein supplements, every price a ₹1,250–4,800 tub. There was no chocolate bar in their world at all — so they couldn't weigh the comparison the ad was asking for.

This world has both halves: the supplement shelf **and** the everyday shelf people actually choose between.

| | old world | this world |
|---|---|---|
| brands | 20, all supplements | **45** |
| cheapest thing in it | ₹1,250 tub | **₹10 biscuit pack** |
| confectionery | none | Dairy Milk, KitKat, Snickers, 5 Star, Perk, Munch, Silk, Bournville |
| biscuits & namkeen | none | Parle-G, Good Day, Marie, Bourbon, Dark Fantasy, Oreo, Lay's, Haldiram's, Bikaji |
| sourced market facts | 0 | **20** |

The old world is untouched and still there. It's the control we compare against.

## ⭐ The finding that matters most

**A protein bar was never on the impulse shelf.**

Against a ₹17 Dairy Milk, a protein bar is about **2.5× per gram** and roughly **5× per unit**. But the sharper problem is that most better-for-you brands don't sell single bars at all — they sell boxes of 5 to 12. So the real minimum spend is **₹400–950**, against a ₹10–17 coin.

At ₹80–125, a protein bar isn't competing with a chocolate bar. It's competing with a 400g family tin of Haldiram's (₹110) or a 230g Dark Fantasy (₹205) — **a household's snack, not one person's treat.** Different occasion, different mental account.

That reframes the last result. The ad may not have failed at persuasion. It may have been asking for a kind of purchase its audience doesn't make in that moment.

## ⭐ The second finding: impulse is no longer impulsive

Kantar India, 2025:

> *"over 75% of purchases in traditional impulse categories — chocolates, confectioneries, cookies, salty snacks, and fizzy drinks — are now planned before entering a store"*
>
> *"the moment of impulse has moved from the store to the screen"*

Quick-commerce data says the same from the other end: sessions under 5 minutes, search-led, eight times the conversion of ordinary e-commerce, very few pages viewed. People arrive knowing what they want.

**This is good news for what we do** — if the decision is made on a feed days earlier, the ad genuinely is the deciding moment rather than the shelf.

⚠ **One honest caveat:** Kantar publishes no sample size, panel or period for that 75%. It's the most quotable and least checkable number we have. If you have your own read on it, we'd rather use yours.

## The moments people snack in

These eight occasions are **ours** — we wrote them. They are not anyone's published segmentation.

| moment | what it competes with | where |
|---|---|---|
| 4pm dip at a desk | office jar, canteen samosa, chai, nothing | pantry, desk drawer, shop downstairs |
| late-night craving | chocolate, ice cream, leftover mithai | ten-minute app, kitchen cupboard |
| post-workout | shake, eggs, banana, dal-chawal | gym counter, specialist online |
| breakfast on the run | toast, poha, cereal, skipping | kitchen counter, station kiosk |
| guilt-free treat | dark chocolate, "healthy" dessert, regular chocolate anyway | app, supermarket, cafe |
| travel & commute | sandwich, chips, nuts, counter chocolate | airport, highway stop, packed from home |
| daily health routine | powder, gummies, multivitamin, real food | subscription, pharmacy |
| household stock-up | biscuit packs, namkeen, whatever kids will eat | supermarket, kirana |

⚠ **We do not know how big any of these are relative to each other.** We searched specifically and established that nobody publishes occasion sizes for India — that data sits inside Kantar's commercial panel and paywalled reports. Right now all eight are treated as equally sized, which is almost certainly wrong. **This is the single most valuable thing you could correct.**

## The 20 facts behind it

The full list with sources, dates and the exact sentence each came from is in the pack file. The ones that shape behaviour most:

**On the market**
- Savoury snacks ₹796bn, growing ~11% — and still only **57% organised**. Packaged food overall is **81% unorganised**. *(Frost & Sullivan, in a SEBI-filed IPO document)*
- **Availability, not preference, decides what gets bought.** Britannia reaches 2.87m outlets directly against a ~9m universe; Bikaji reaches 0.33m. *(company filings)*
- GST on snacks was cut to 5% in Sept 2025 — and it **broke market-share measurement.** Britannia has stopped publishing its biscuit share entirely. No 2025-26 biscuit share is quotable, from anyone.

**On shoppers**
- 41% of Indian adults snack daily; 22% use snacks to replace a meal. *(Mondelez/Mintel 2026)*
- **There is no national daypart curve.** Two Indian cities came out nearly inverted — one 96.7% evening, another 85.2% morning. When people snack is a *city* question, not a country one.
- **Bought outside, eaten at home.** 71% of value transacts out-of-home, but 78–94% of snacks are eaten at home. Both true, different things.
- **The eater is often not the chooser** — youth 34%, children 27%, adults 22% of household snacking decisions.
- Kirana isn't simply losing. Traditional trade **gained** share in out-of-home salty snacks, 31%→35%. The occasion splits the channel.

**On health claims**
- India's own nutrition authority states that fortifying a processed food **"cannot make them wholesome or healthy"** — and separately advises against protein supplements for muscle. At their threshold of 3g added sugar per 100g, **every sweet packaged snack including the healthy bars is HFSS.**
- ⚠ **The protein-deficiency statistic everyone quotes is industry-funded.** "Right To Protein" states on its own site that it's *"powered by the U.S. Soybean Export Council."* The academic position is that Indian protein *quantity* is roughly adequate and the real gap is *quality*. **We should stop repeating the 73% figure**, and the existing supplement pack carries a claim from the same family that needs re-checking.
- **Health framing can repel the buyer it targets** — taste-focused labels beat health-focused ones by 29% in a preregistered study of 137,842 real decisions, working through *taste expectations*. ⚠ But that's American, the effect **reverses in France**, and nobody has tested it in India. A hypothesis to test, not a finding to assert.
- Indians barely read labels — taste, price and celebrity endorsement outrank them.
- **Front-of-pack labelling is still not law.** Draft since 2022; the Supreme Court gave the government two weeks on 13 Aug 2026. Live and moving.

**On prices**
- At ₹5 and ₹10 you cannot raise price, so **the pack shrinks instead.** Standard pack sizes stopped being mandated in Oct 2022.
- People experience this as **less biscuit, not more money** — and respond by moving to loose/unbranded product rather than paying more. Parle's own words: buyers *"would rather buy something else… something that is loose, not coming from the organised sector."*

## What we don't know — your agenda

These are gaps, stated plainly. Each is something you may already have.

1. **How people actually talk about snacks.** Our voice samples cover supplements only. The snacking quote collection failed — every consumer platform blocks automated access — and we chose to return nothing rather than invent quotes, because a fake quote is indistinguishable from a real one once it's inside a persona. **Your social listening would fix this immediately, and it's the single most useful thing you could hand us.**
2. **Occasion sizes.** How big is the afternoon slump against late-night against the monthly shop, in *your* category?
3. **Daypart by city.** Which cities do you actually sell in, and when do they snack?
4. **Category sizes** for chocolate, biscuits, ice cream and protein bars — never sourced at citable quality. Only savoury snacks has a defensible public number.
5. **The D2C better-for-you cohort** — Whole Truth, MuscleBlaze, RiteBite, Farmley — no sourced revenue, share or repeat data anywhere.
6. **Trial-to-repeat rates.** No public data exists. You know yours.
7. **Your channel split** — quick commerce vs kirana vs modern trade.
8. **The unverified prices** listed above. You could confirm these in minutes.
9. **Does the health-framing effect hold in India?** Strong evidence, entirely non-Indian.
10. **Front-of-pack labelling** — re-check before this goes to any client.

## Two things to be clear about

**This is a new, untested world.** Only the original supplement world has been validated against a real run. An ad outside a tested category doesn't fail gracefully — it produces a confident answer that's wrong. Running against this pack is a first, not a certification.

**Nothing has been run and nothing committed.** The world exists; no money has been spent using it.
