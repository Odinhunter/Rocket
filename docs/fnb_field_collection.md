# Field collection — five real days, five kinds of person

**For the user to fill in, 2026-08-20.** Blank tables below. Bring them back filled and they go
into `docs/fnb_real_events.md` and settle decisions 3 and 4 on real data.

---

## HOW TO ASK — three rules, and the first one we learned the hard way

### 1. ⭐⭐ ASK ABOUT **YESTERDAY**, NEVER ABOUT "A TYPICAL DAY"

We already have proof this matters, from you. Your **actual** day had upma, a canteen lunch,
office samosas and a Diet Coke. Your **ideal** day had seeds, paneer, Greek yogurt, edamame and
tempeh. **Same person, same week, almost nothing in common.**

Ask *"a typical day"* and people describe the day they wish they had. So ask:

> **"Walk me through yesterday, from when you woke up to when you slept. Everything you ate or
> drank, including tea, water-bottle purchases, anything someone handed you."**

If yesterday was odd (fasting, travelling, ill, a festival), that's fine — **write it down and
note why.** An odd day is real data. A tidied-up day is not.

### 2. ⭐ ONE ROW PER **ITEM**, NOT PER MEAL

If someone has tea *and* two biscuits, that's **two rows**. If they have breakfast *and* take a
tablet with it, that's **two rows**. Things eaten together are often two completely different
decisions, and merging them loses the interesting one every time.

### 3. ⚠⚠ DON'T ASK **WHY**, AND DON'T LET THEM SUMMARISE

No *"why did you pick that"*, no *"what kind of snacker are you"*, no *"do you care about
health"*. People answer those with a story about themselves, and the story is usually flattering
and usually wrong. **Only ask what happened, where, and what it cost.** We work out the rest.

⚠ Same rule for you when you write it down: **record their words, not your interpretation.** If
they say *"just some namkeen"*, write *"just some namkeen"* — don't upgrade it to a brand.

# THE FIVE PEOPLE, AND WHY EACH ONE

⭐ **These five are chosen to be as UNLIKE you as possible**, on purpose. Your two days are both
from a protein-focused vegetarian metro office worker. If every day we collect looks like that,
we'll have built our own blind spot into the evidence and then confirmed it.

⭐⭐ **The two things all five are really testing**, because our own testing says these are what
drive everything:
1. **Does a smaller income mean fewer moments in the day?** Our simulated people said yes,
   strongly — the three highest earners had every moment, the two lowest had almost none. **That
   has never been checked against a real person.** It decides how big the map needs to be.
2. **Which moments does the person actually control?** Your day showed the office decided your
   lunch and your evening snack. A brand can't win a moment somebody else provides.

---

## PERSON 1 — A WOMAN WHO RUNS A HOUSEHOLD

**Roughly 40-55, small town or mid-size city, no paid job outside the home.**

⭐⭐ **Why she's first: she is the only kind of person we have actually built.** We generated 192
of her, you read them and said they weren't representative, and **not one real day of hers has
ever been checked.** Everything we believe about that region is currently unverified.

She also answers something no one else can: **she is the one who provides.** Your day had food
that appeared without you buying it. She's the other side of that — the person deciding what
appears for everyone else.

**Especially worth capturing:** anything she made or bought *for someone else*, and anything she
ate standing up or out of a container while cooking.

## PERSON 2 — SOMEONE WHOSE WORK IS PHYSICAL, OR ON A SHIFT

**25-45. Factory floor, site work, delivery rider, driver, security guard, shop counter, kirana.
Paid daily or monthly, but not sitting at a desk.**

⭐⭐ **Why: this is where our biggest untested claim lives.** Our simulated people said that
someone on a small income simply *doesn't have* most of the moments in the day — no mid-morning
break, no late-night order, no daily supplement. **If that's true, the map we're arguing about is
far smaller and cheaper than we think. If it's false, we've been about to build something wrong.**

⚠ And a second thing: **our whole model assumes an office clock.** One of our moments is literally
called *"the 4pm dip at a desk"*. A shift worker's day may not have a 4pm at all — and India's own
data says the eating curve flips between cities.

**Especially worth capturing:** what time their shift started and ended, and whether food came from
a canteen, a cart, home, or nothing.

## PERSON 3 — SOMEONE YOUNG, 18-24

**Student, or first job. Lives with family or shares a room. Their own spending money is small even
if the household's isn't.**

⭐ **Why: they are who most food advertising is actually aimed at**, they snack more than anyone
else, and we have **zero** evidence about them. They're also the most likely to have the moments
our model is weakest on — late at night, on the move, ordering on an app at odd hours.

**Especially worth capturing:** anything ordered on an app, anything eaten after 10pm, and whether
they paid or someone else did.

## PERSON 4 — SOMEONE WHO DOESN'T CARE ABOUT HEALTH AT ALL

**Any age. Comfortable enough to choose. Eats what they like. ⭐ Non-vegetarian if you can.**

⭐⭐ **Why this one matters most, and it's the least obvious: both days we have are from someone
optimising for protein.** Our own rule is that a panel where everybody likes the product measures
nothing — and we're at risk of doing exactly that with our real-world evidence.

**We need someone who would find a protein bar faintly ridiculous.** Someone whose 4pm is a samosa
because it's a samosa. That person is the majority, and right now they're missing from everything
we hold.

⚠ **Don't screen for this by asking.** Nobody says *"I don't care about health"*. Pick someone you
already know eats freely, and just take their day.

## PERSON 5 — SOMEONE MANAGING A HEALTH CONDITION

**50+. Diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol, a recent scare — something where a doctor told them
to change what they eat.**

⭐ **Why: this is the only person whose "daily routine" is genuinely non-negotiable**, and it's a
large and growing part of the Indian market. They'll have real daily tablets, real restrictions,
and real substitutions that were forced rather than chosen.

⭐ They also test the one thing that separates a *restriction* from a *preference* — our model
currently can't tell those apart, and they behave completely differently in front of an ad.

**Especially worth capturing:** what they stopped eating and what replaced it, and anything they
take daily at a fixed time.

# THE TABLES

⚠ **Columns are deliberately plain.** Nothing here uses our internal words for moments or buyer
types — if the table nudges someone into our categories, we've collected our own assumptions back.

**Column guide:**
- **Time** — roughly is fine. *"After the shift"* is fine.
- **What they had** — as specifically as they say it. Brand if it was packaged, dish if cooked.
- **Where** — home, office, street, shop, bus, someone else's house.
- **Bought / made / given** — ⭐ who paid, and if it was provided free, say so.
- **With anyone?** — eaten with people, or given to someone else.
- **Nearly had instead?** — only if they volunteer it. Don't push.

# WHAT HAPPENS TO THESE

1. Each item gets sorted, by hand, into both the **current model** and the **proposed map** — the
   same way your day was, and the routings for your day are already locked and dated so they can't
   be quietly bent to fit.
2. **Count how many moments each person's day actually contains.** That is the direct test of the
   income claim, and it decides how big the map should be.
3. **Count which items neither model can place.** Your day produced four of those in one day.
4. ⚠ **What five days still can't do:** they can't measure how *common* anything is. Five people
   is a shape, not a rate. It's enough to decide the map's structure, not to size it.

⭐ **If any of the five is hard to find, tell me and I'll say what to substitute** — some of these
five are far more load-bearing than others, and Person 2 and Person 4 are the two I'd protect.
