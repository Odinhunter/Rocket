# Lane 5 — Health narrative and regulation

**Scope:** Indian urban snacking — the health/regulatory backdrop.
**Research date:** 14 August 2026. **Regulatory status is live and moving — see the dated timeline below.**
**Rule applied:** every number below carries a source, a period, a URL and a verbatim sentence. Anything I could not source is in **Open Questions**, not in the body.

## Key findings

1. **Front-of-pack labelling in India is still NOT law.** The Indian Nutrition Rating (INR) star scheme has been a *draft* since 13 September 2022 and has never been notified. As of 13 August 2026 the Supreme Court gave the Union **two weeks** to place a final decision on record. This is the single most checkable fact in this lane: **INR is draft, not notified, not mandatory.**
2. **The state's own nutrition science is hostile to the entire "healthier snack" proposition.** ICMR-NIN's Dietary Guidelines (2024) explicitly say fortifying or enriching an ultra-processed/HFSS food *cannot* make it healthy — which is exactly the mechanism most "added protein / added fibre" snacks rely on.
3. **ICMR-NIN also tells consumers not to buy protein powders.** Guideline 9 reads, in part, "avoid protein supplements to build muscle mass." The protein boom is running directly against the national dietary guideline.
4. **The most-quoted protein-gap statistic is industry-funded.** Right To Protein states on its own site that it is "powered by the U.S. Soybean Export Council (USSEC)." The credible academic position (Kurpad et al., *Br J Nutr*) is that Indian protein *quantity* is roughly adequate (~1 g/kg/day) and the real deficit is *quality* — with about **one third** of the sedentary rural population at risk, not 73%.
5. **On the live hypothesis (health framing repelling treat buyers): the literature genuinely supports it, with an important caveat.** The strongest evidence is Turnwald et al. 2019 (*Psychological Science*, 137,842 decisions): taste-focused labels increased selection **29% vs health-focused labels**. Mediation ran through *taste expectations*. **But** the underlying "unhealthy = tasty" intuition is **culturally learned and reverses in some countries** (France), and **there is no India-specific experimental evidence either way**. See the flagged subsection.
6. **Indian consumers do not read labels much, and are already sceptical of claims.** A 32-study scoping review (Global Health Action, 2025) found taste, price, peer influence and celebrity endorsement are "stronger drivers of food choice than food labels."

**The industry's stated defence, on the record (13 Aug 2026).** FSSAI's Additional Solicitor General argued that Indian traditional foods naturally contain higher salt, fat and sugar than international thresholds, so warning labels would put red symbols on nearly all traditional Indian foods, stigmatising them and harming MSME revenues. *This is the live public argument against warning labels and it is a nationalist/livelihood argument, not a nutritional one — worth knowing when modelling how an Indian consumer talks about "junk food" labelling.*

**Verbatim — Parliamentary Standing Committee, 24 July 2026** (ThePrint):
> "The absence of simple interpretative tools such as warning labels or colour-coded indicators limits the effectiveness of such disclosures in guiding consumer behaviour."
> "clear, prominent front-of-pack warnings, particularly on products high in sugar, could help consumers make informed choices by providing simple, easy-to-understand information at the point of purchase."

### The controversy: how the star rating was chosen

The Reporters' Collective investigation "A Star-Crossed Label" (5 Oct 2025, by Gayatri Sapru) documents the industry-influence account. Verbatim:
> "Industry representatives routinely outnumbered consumer groups in ratios of six to one, giving food giants like PepsiCo, Coca-Cola and Haldiram a chance to sit at the table and influence food safety policy."

On the commissioned IIM-Ahmedabad study (FSSAI paid ~₹2.6 crore) that underpinned the choice of stars over warnings, the piece reports the study
> "showed warning labels FOPL system deterred purchases more effectively, yet its conclusion pivoted to something entirely different and more industry-friendly, citing 'ease of understanding'."

Civil-society criticism recorded in the same piece: the INR system allows "up to 3.5 times more sugar" than WHO recommendations and, through fortification loopholes, creates a "healthy halo to an otherwise unhealthy food."

> ⚠ **This matters for our work.** Indian public-health critics are using the phrase *health halo* explicitly, and the regulator's own commissioned research reportedly found warning labels deter purchase more than stars. That is a second, independent line of evidence that **health framing changes purchase behaviour in India** — though it is deterrence of an unhealthy product, not suppression of a healthy one.

### What Indian consumers say they want from a label

Bhattacharya S, Bera OP, Shah V. "Consumers' Perception About Front of Package Food Labels (FOPL) in India: A Survey of 14 States." *Front Public Health* 2022;10:936802. n = 2,024 across 14 states.
> "Warning Labels (WL) were the most preferred food labels (93%), followed by Multiple Traffic Lights (MTL) and the difference between the two was statistically significant (p-value < 0.05)."

95% of participants were aware of food package labelling and "88.6% of them considered this information helpful." *(Note: stated preference and awareness — not observed behaviour. Contrast with the scoping review below, which finds label use is low in practice.)*

### HFSS and advertising to children

- **The operative definition of HFSS in India is ICMR-NIN's, not a notified FOPNL threshold.** See the thresholds table in the next section.
- **School-adjacent restrictions are being enforced at state level in 2026.** Maharashtra's FDA issued a compliance order on 27 July 2026 banning sale, advertisement and free distribution of HFSS foods within school premises and up to 50 metres from the school compound, covering roughly 1.08 lakh schools and ~2 crore students. Karnataka has a comparable prohibition within a 50-metre radius of schools and colleges.
- I could **not** verify the exact title and current amendment status of the underlying central regulation (see Open Questions). The FSSAI school-children regulations and the Food Safety and Standards (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018 are the instruments usually cited, but I did not open an authoritative text, so I am not asserting their current wording.

## The nutrition science backdrop (ICMR-NIN, INDIAB)

### ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians, 2024 — direct quotes

Source: ICMR–National Institute of Nutrition, *Dietary Guidelines for Indians*, 2024, 148 pp. **I downloaded and extracted the full PDF; all quotes below are verbatim from it.**

**Guideline 15 — "Minimize the consumption of high fat, sugar, salt (HFSS) and ultra-processed foods (UPFs)"** (p. 93). Rationale, verbatim:
> "Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) are often high in fat, sugar and salt (HFSS). Regular consumption of UPFs or HFSS are known to increase the risk of non-communicable diseases like diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, etc."

**On sugar** (p. 111), verbatim:
> "Consumption of sugar in quantities that contribute over 5% of total energy intake per day or 25 g/day (based on average intake of 2000 Kcal/day) is defined as 'high' sugar. WHO is considering revising its recommendation and reducing calories from sugar to <5% Kcal/day. Limiting sugar to 25g/day is better for health. If possible, added sugar may be completely eliminated from one's diet as it adds no nutritive value other than calories. Calories are healthy only when accompanied by vitamins, minerals and fibre."

Elsewhere the guidelines state flatly: **"Sugar should be less than 5% of the total energy per day."** and **"No added sugar for children <2 years old."**

**Definition of added sugars**, verbatim — note that it explicitly captures *jaggery and honey*, which matters enormously for Indian "natural sweetener" positioning:
> "Added sugars refer to sugars and sugar syrups added to foods and drinks during processing and preparation and they include sucrose (table sugar), jaggery, honey, glucose, fructose, dextrose etc. Adding sugar over and above what is naturally/inherently present in foods increases the total calorie intake, but adds no nutritive value. Refined extracted sugars have no vitamins or minerals."

**On why UPFs are unhealthy**, verbatim:
> "The lack of fibre and poor micronutrient content make them unhealthy. Additionally, UPFs contribute to high calorie intake as they are often high in fat or sugar or both. These foods are widely consumed because they offer unique taste, high palatability, low cost, and easy availability, even in remote areas."

> ⚠ **The single most commercially damaging sentence in the document** (p. 113), verbatim:
> **"If the foods are ultra-processed or high in fat/sugar/salt, then enriching them with nutrients or fortifying cannot make them wholesome or healthy."**
> This is the national nutrition authority pre-emptively rejecting the "we added protein/fibre/vitamins to a snack" playbook.

**The HFSS threshold table** (Table 15.1, "Nutrients of concern threshold criteria for foods and beverages"), verbatim values per 100 g solid / 100 ml liquid:

| Nutrient | Liquids (per 100 ml) | Solids (per 100 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calorie (kcal) | 70 | 250 |
| Salt (mg) | 175 | 625 |
| Added sugar (g) | 2.0 | 3 |
| Added fat (g) | 1.5 | 4.2 |

Verbatim footnotes: for solid foods, "Threshold for sugar has been calculated at ~5% energy from added sugar, and not exceeding 10% energy from total sugar" and "Threshold for fat has been calculated at ~15% energy from added fat, and not exceeding 30% energy from total fat."

Also verbatim: "The intake of salt above 5g/day (sodium >2g/day) is considered 'high'." And HFSS is defined as "a food or diet that contains more than 15 percent of energy from any cooking vegetable oils or ghee, butter (visible or added oil/fat), etc."

> **Practical implication:** at 3 g added sugar per 100 g, essentially every sweet packaged Indian snack — including most "healthy" bars, laddoos and granolas — is HFSS by ICMR-NIN's own threshold.

**Guideline 17 — "Read information on food labels to make informed and healthy food choices"** (p. 125) contains an explicit list of claims the guidelines call misleading. Verbatim on the two most relevant to snacking:
> "'Sugar free': People often associate sugar-free foods with lower calories and perceive these to be beneficial for diabetics and weight watchers. Sugar-free foods may be loaded with fats, refined cereals (white flour, starch) and even hidden sugars (maltitol, fructose, corn syrup, molasses). These would imply high glycaemic index and high calories in the food item."

> "'Made with whole grain': These words could be misinterpreted. Such a statement does not necessarily imply that the food items are not tertiary or ultra-processed."

> "'Good source of protein, vitamin D' or other nutrients': A good source means that a single serving of that food has 10% to 19% of the amount of that nutrient. While this information is useful, read about the quantity of these nutrients with reference to daily nutrient recommended."

> "'All natural': A food product can be called 'natural' if it doesn't have added colors, added flavors or artificial substances and goes through minimal processing. This term is often used rather loosely. It is often used by manufacturers to identify one or two natural ingredients in the mix and this can be misleading."

## The protein-gap narrative and its critics

### The claim, and who is publishing it

**"73% of Indian diets are protein deficient"** originates from a 2017 IMRB survey. The most-cited vehicle for protein-gap messaging in India is the **Right To Protein** campaign. Its funding is stated on its own website, verbatim:

> **"Right to Protein is a public health and nutrition initiative powered by the U.S. Soybean Export Council (USSEC)."**

> ⚠ **This is a soy-industry export-promotion body.** The campaign's stated mission — "Our mission is to put protein at the forefront of the global nutrition agenda" — is an advocacy mission, not a research one. A brand citing the 73% figure is citing a market-research survey commissioned within an industry-funded awareness campaign. **A brand research team will find this in one search.**

A second widely circulated figure — "nine out of ten Indians consume a protein-deficient diet," from a 2020 IMRB study "Understanding Protein Myths & Gaps among Indians" (n = 1,800) — comes from the same campaign lineage. I could not open a primary methodology document for either survey (see Open Questions).

### The credible academic counterweight

Swaminathan S, Vaz M, Kurpad AV. "Protein intakes in India." *British Journal of Nutrition* 2012;108:S50–S58, doi:10.1017/S0007114512002413. **I downloaded and extracted the full PDF; this is the verbatim abstract:**

> "Indian diets derive almost 60 % of their protein from cereals with relatively low digestibility and quality. There have been several surveys of diets and protein intakes in India by the National Nutrition Monitoring Board (NNMB) over the last 25 years, in urban and rural, as well as in slum dwellers and tribal populations. **Data of disadvantaged populations from slums, tribals and sedentary rural Indian populations show that the protein intake (mainly from cereals) is about 1 gm/kg/day.** However, the protein intake looks less promising in terms of the protein digestibility corrected amino acid score (PDCAAS), using lysine as the first limiting amino acid, where all populations, particularly rural and tribal, appear to have an inadequate quality to their protein intake. The protein: energy (PE) ratio is a measure of dietary quality, and has been used in the 2007 WHO/FAO/UNU report to define reference requirement values with which the adequacy of diets can be evaluated in terms of a protein quality corrected PE ratio. **It is likely that about one third of this sedentary rural population is at risk of not meeting their requirements.** These levels of risk of deficiency are in a population with relatively low BMI populations, whose diets are also inadequate in fruits and vegetables. Therefore, while the burden of enhancing the quality of protein intake in rural India exists, the quality of the diet, in general, represents a challenge that must be met."

**The even-handed read:**
- The adult RDA is **0.83 g/kg/day** (safe level; EAR 0.66 g/kg/day) per WHO/FAO/UNU 2007, adopted by ICMR. Measured intake in *disadvantaged* Indian populations is **~1 g/kg/day** — i.e. **protein quantity clears the bar**.
- The genuine deficit is **quality** (lysine-limited, cereal-dominated, PDCAAS-corrected), and on a quality-corrected basis roughly **one third** of a sedentary rural population is at risk.
- So: **there is a real protein problem in India, and it is not the one the marketing says it is.** It is a protein-*quality* and overall-diet-*quality* problem concentrated in rural and low-income populations — not a 73% deficiency in the urban packaged-snack buyer, who is the person actually being sold a protein bar.

### What ICMR-NIN itself says about protein supplements

**Guideline 9** (2024 guidelines) is titled, in part: **"Obtain good quality proteins and essential amino acids (EAA) through appropriate combination of foods and avoid protein supplements to build muscle mass."** Verbatim from the body:

> "Consuming high level of protein, especially in the form of protein supplement powders, is therefore not advisable."

> "Taking protein supplements on top of normal meal (balanced diet) intake is not going to build more muscle."

> "Whey protein is rich in branched chain amino acids (BCAAs). Recent evidence suggests that BCAAs may increase the risk of certain non-communicable diseases (NCDs)."

> "Protein requirements are not as high as commonly perceived. In fact, research findings indicate that dietary protein supplementation is associated with only a small increase in muscle strength and size during prolonged resistance exercise training (RET) in healthy adults; and protein intake levels greater than ~1.6g/kg/day do not contribute any further to RET-induced gains in muscle mass."

"Points to register" for that guideline, verbatim:
> "Most athletes can get the recommended amount of protein through appropriate selection of food items. Protein powders are unnecessary for normal healthy life."
> "Indiscriminate use of excess protein as supplements for prolonged periods can affect health."

Also verbatim, relevant to vegetarian positioning:
> "Vegans eating varied diets containing vegetables, beans, grains, nuts, and seeds will have no difficulty in obtaining enough protein from regular diet."
> "Appropriate combination of cereals with pulses in the ratio of 3:1 (raw) is the most efficient way to obtain all the nine essential amino acids from a vegan diet."

## Health claims, taste expectations and the health halo ← the important one

**The live hypothesis being tested:** *a health promise can repel the very buyer it targets when the occasion is a treat.*

**Verdict up front: the literature substantially supports the mechanism, the effect is well-replicated at scale in field settings, and the mediator is exactly the one the hypothesis names (taste expectation). But two serious qualifications apply — the intuition is culturally learned and reverses in some populations, and there is no India-specific experimental evidence.**

### 1. The foundational finding — "unhealthy = tasty"

Raghunathan R, Naylor RW, Hoyer WD. "The Unhealthy = Tasty Intuition and Its Effects on Taste Inferences, Enjoyment, and Choice of Food Products." *Journal of Marketing* 2006;70(4):170–184, doi:10.1509/jmkg.70.4.170.

Verbatim from the published abstract:
> "when information pertaining to the assessment of the healthiness of food items is provided, the less healthy the item is portrayed to be, the better is its inferred taste, the more it is enjoyed during actual consumption, and the greater is the preference for it in choice tasks when a hedonic goal is more salient."

Two details matter for our purposes. First, **the effect held even among people who deny holding the belief** — the authors "obtained these effects both among consumers who report that they believe healthiness and tastiness are negatively correlated and, to a lesser degree, among those who do not report such a belief," and showed the association "operates at an implicit level." *A survey asking Indian consumers "do you think healthy food tastes worse?" will therefore understate this.* Second, the choice effect is conditioned on **"when a hedonic goal is more salient"** — which is precisely the treat/indulgence occasion in the hypothesis.

An open-access review (Front. Nutr. / PMC9563033) characterises the finding verbatim as: US Americans "implicitly associate unhealthy food with tastiness," and reports that consumers with strong unhealthy-tasty beliefs "expected the healthy product to be healthier but less tasty than the unhealthy product," with the belief "positively related only to the negative emotions associated with the healthy product: disgust ... and boredom."

### 2. The strongest evidence that health framing suppresses choice — at scale, in the field

Turnwald BP, Boles DZ, Crum AJ. "Association Between Indulgent Descriptions and Vegetable Consumption: Twisted Carrots and Dynamite Beets." *JAMA Intern Med* 2017;177(8):1216–1218, doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2017.1637. 27,933 diners, 8,279 vegetable selections, 46 weekdays.

Identical vegetables, four label framings. Verbatim results:
> indulgent labelling drew "25% more people selecting the vegetable than in the basic condition (95% CI, 1%-49%; P = .04)"
> "41% more people than in the healthy restrictive condition (95% CI, 18%-64%; P = .001)"
> "35% more people than in the healthy positive condition (95% CI, 10%-60%; P = .01)"
> "33% increase in mass of vegetables consumed compared with the healthy restrictive condition (95% CI, 11%-53%; P = .004)"

> **Read that carefully: "healthy positive" framing (e.g. "vitamin-rich corn") performed WORSE than plain naming and much worse than indulgent framing — for the identical food.** Positive health framing, not just restrictive framing, suppressed selection.

Then the preregistered, multi-site replication — **the single best citation for this hypothesis**:

Turnwald BP, Bertoldo JD, Perry MA, Policastro P, Timmons M, Bosso C, Connors P, Valgenti RT, Pine L, Challamel G, Gardner CD, Crum AJ. "Increasing Vegetable Intake by Emphasizing Tasty and Enjoyable Attributes: A Randomized Controlled Multisite Intervention for Taste-Focused Labeling." *Psychological Science* 2019;30(11):1603–1615, doi:10.1177/0956797619872191.

Verbatim abstract:
> "Healthy food labels tout health benefits, yet most people prioritize tastiness in the moment of food choice. In a preregistered intervention, we tested whether taste-focused labels compared with health-focused labels increased vegetable intake at five university dining halls throughout the United States. Across 137,842 diner decisions, 185 days, and 24 vegetable types, taste-focused labels increased vegetable selection by 29% compared with health-focused labels and by 14% compared with basic labels. Vegetable consumption also increased."

And the mechanism, verbatim: **"Increased expectations of a positive taste experience mediated the effect"** of taste-focused labelling on selection.

> **This is the load-bearing citation.** 137,842 real decisions, preregistered, multi-site, randomised. Health framing lost to taste framing by 29%, and it lost *through taste expectations*. That is the hypothesis's exact mechanism, empirically identified.

### 3. The health halo — and its reverse

The classic "health halo" runs the other way: a health cue causes *over*-consumption and calorie underestimation. Both directions are real and they are not contradictory — the halo governs **how much you eat once you've chosen**; the taste-expectation effect governs **whether you choose it at all**.

For the negative/reverse halo specifically, the relevant construct is documented as: health-related claims can *lower* purchase intention through anticipated loss of hedonic benefit — reported in the literature on nutrition claims applied to **hedonic vs utilitarian** foods (see e.g. *Food Quality and Preference*, S0950329317301994, on self-health awareness, perceived benefits and purchase intention for hedonic vs utilitarian foods with nutrition claims). **I did not open this paper's full text (paywalled) — treat the specific framing as indicative, and cite Turnwald 2019 instead when a hard number is needed.**

### 4. ⚠ Evidence AGAINST the hypothesis (the task asked for this — here it is)

**(a) The intuition is culturally learned and it REVERSES in France.**
Werle COC, Trendel O, Ardito G. "Unhealthy food is not tastier for everybody: The 'healthy = tasty' French intuition." *Food Quality and Preference* 2013;28(1):116–121.

The review at PMC9563033 characterises it verbatim: among French consumers, "unhealthy food is associated with bad taste and healthy food with tastiness," and contextualises: "In the USA, a utilitarian view of food consumption predominates ... whereas in France, food is often associated with pleasure." The original reports that a neutral food *described as healthy* was rated tastier, more pleasurable and of better quality than the same food described as unhealthy. The authors conclude the health–pleasure dichotomy "is learnt, at least in part, through one's cultural background."

> **This is the strongest single caution.** The unhealthy=tasty intuition is a *US* finding. India is not the US and is not France. Assuming the US direction holds for an Indian consumer is an unvalidated transfer.

**(b) The effect is moderated by how the food is categorised.**
Mai R. et al., "Food categorization determines whether healthier food is inferred to be tastier or less tasty," *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 2025, doi:10.1002/jcpy.1447 — the direction of the health→taste inference depends on the food category frame. *(Located but not opened; cited as a pointer, not as evidence.)*

**(c) Food-pleasure orientation attenuates it.** "Food pleasure orientation diminishes the 'healthy = less tasty' intuition" (*Food Quality and Preference*, S095032931630146X) — individual differences moderate the effect. *(Located, not opened.)*

**(d) Indian consumers state a preference for warning labels (93%, 14-state survey above) and 88.6% call label information helpful.** That is stated preference for *more* health information, not less — though stated preference and treat-occasion behaviour are different things, and the scoping review below shows the gap.

### 5. What this genuinely supports for our work

**Supported:**
- Health framing *can* reduce selection relative to taste framing, at scale, in real purchase settings, mediated by taste expectations (Turnwald 2019 — 29%).
- Even *positive* health framing underperforms plain naming (Turnwald 2017).
- The effect is strongest "when a hedonic goal is more salient" (Raghunathan 2006) — i.e. exactly the treat occasion.
- Consumers holding the belief expect healthy versions to be less tasty and report *disgust and boredom* toward them.

**Not supported / not established:**
- That this holds **in India**. There is no India-specific experimental evidence in either direction that I could source.
- That "no added sugar" specifically triggers it. The experimental literature uses health framing broadly; I found **no** experiment isolating a "no added sugar" or "guilt-free" claim's effect on taste expectation and purchase.
- Any effect size for Indian packaged snacks. The 29% figure is US university dining halls and vegetables — a very different choice architecture from a ₹40 impulse snack purchase.

> **Honest framing for the pack:** treat this as a *well-evidenced mechanism with unestablished Indian magnitude and direction*. It is a strong hypothesis to test, not a finding to assert.

## Consumer skepticism and label-reading

**The best India-specific evidence.** Pahlani M, Talati KN, Lopez-Arana S, Narayanan P. "Food labelling in India: a scoping review of consumer engagement, comprehension, and purchase behaviour." *Global Health Action* 2025, doi:10.1080/16549716.2025.2574132. **32 studies, India-only, 2014–2024.**

Verbatim findings:
> "Only a small proportion of the general population reported consistent use of labels" — with participants checking "most frequently expiry dates" rather than nutrition information.
> "Few studies reported that although participants recognised nutrition terms or FOP symbols, they often could not interpret them correctly."
> "Scepticism toward nutrition and health claims was frequently reported as a barrier to comprehension and use."
> **"Respondents frequently cited taste, price, peer influence, media exposure, and celebrity endorsements as stronger drivers of food choice than food labels."**

The review names a persistent **"intention–action gap in food label use"** — awareness does not convert to healthier purchasing.

> ⚠ **This is directly relevant to the hypothesis, and it cuts against over-weighting label claims in either direction.** If taste and price dominate label information for Indian consumers, then both the *positive* effect of a health claim and the *negative* taste-expectation effect may be smaller in India than the US literature implies — because the label is being read less in the first place. The two India findings (low label use; high stated preference for warning labels) are in tension, and that tension is itself a finding.

**Claims are concentrated on unhealthy products.** A 2025 analysis of nutrition and health claims on Indian packaged foods (*CyTA – Journal of Food*, doi:10.1080/19476337.2025.2520543) reports higher usage of nutrition and health claims on unhealthy compared with healthy product categories. **The publisher page returned HTTP 403 and I could not extract verbatim text or the exact product sample — cite with caution or verify before use.**

**Protein-claim integrity:** see Citizens Protein Project 2 above (83% of pharma-marketed whey powders mislabelled beyond ±5%; nutraceutical brands matched claims closely).

## One-paragraph brief for persona construction

An urban Indian snack buyer in 2026 lives in a country with 101 million diabetics and 136 million prediabetics, where the national nutrition authority has formally told them that fortifying a processed snack "cannot make them wholesome or healthy," that jaggery and honey count as added sugar, that anything over 3 g added sugar per 100 g is HFSS, and that protein powders are "unnecessary for normal healthy life" — while the Supreme Court publicly accuses the food regulator of "succumbing to pressure" from industry and no warning label has yet appeared on any pack. That consumer reads labels rarely, checks the expiry date more than the nutrition panel, is *sceptical of health claims as a rule*, and chooses on taste, price and who endorsed it. Against that, the international experimental evidence says a health-framed pitch can lose ~29% of selection to a taste-framed pitch for an identical product, through damaged taste expectations — **but that evidence is American, the same intuition runs backwards in France, and nobody has tested it in India.**
