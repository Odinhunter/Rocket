## Detail

### 1. Quick commerce in India, August 2026 — size, growth, share, dark stores, competitive split

**Market size and share of e-retail.** The Bain × Flipkart *How India Shops Online 2026* report is the
best-sourced sizing available. Verbatim:

> "Q-commerce (less than 30-minute delivery): India is a global leader in Q-commerce adoption, with
> 16%–17% of e-retail GMV flowing through Q-commerce platforms—significantly higher than most markets,
> including China, the only other country with breakout Q-commerce growth. India has unique structural
> advantages for this format, including dense urban demand pockets, favorable last-mile economics, and low
> grocery penetration."

> "Q-commerce GMV has doubled annually since 2023, reaching $10–$11 billion in 2025. This surge has driven
> an inflection in grocery, which is historically underpenetrated online. E-grocery penetration has grown
> roughly fivefold, reaching 1.5% of the overall grocery market in 2025. Looking ahead, Q-commerce is
> expected to reach $65–$70 billion by the end of the decade and contribute 45%–50% of incremental e-retail
> GMV, as traditional e-retail continues to anchor the overall e-retail market with 60%–65% share by 2030."

The **metro** number matters far more than the national one for an urban snacking pack:

> "Since Q-commerce's launch in 2020, e-grocery penetration has increased fivefold and now accounts for
> approximately 1.5% of the overall grocery market. In Metro cities—the core market for Q-commerce—share of
> e-grocery has reached 6%–7%."

**Dark stores.** Bain, industry-wide:

> "The micro-fulfillment center network has expanded to over 7,000 centers across more than 200 cities.
> Two-thirds of new center additions have been in the top ten cities. Operating scale has materially
> improved profitability; however, customer adoption and sustainable unit economics remain unproven beyond
> top Metros and Tier 1 cities."

⚠️ **The two industry store counts conflict — do not present them as agreeing.** Bain says "over 7,000"
micro-fulfilment centres for CY2025; Redseer's tracker says ~6,280 dark stores in Jan'26 — a *later* date
and a *smaller* number, roughly 10% apart over overlapping periods. The definitions almost certainly
differ (Bain's "micro-fulfillment center" is a broader term than Redseer's tracked q-commerce dark store),
and **neither is a platform disclosure**. The only store counts that come from filings are Blinkit's
**2,443** and Instamart's **1,171** — those are the ones to trust and to quote.

Redseer's monthly tracker, for the growth rates and the productivity metric:

> "Dark store count increased from ~5,990 in Dec'25 to ~6,280 in Jan'26"
> "In January, Quick Commerce GMV reached ~₹11K crore, growing ~100% YoY, while order volumes climbed ~95%
> YoY to ~7.8 million OPD."
> "Monthly Transacting Users expanded ~95% YoY to ~5.2 crore"
> "PAN-India OPD per dark store improved to ~1,255, up ~15% YoY."
> "Non-grocery grew ~1.6x faster than grocery"

*(Redseer, "Quick Commerce finds its new normal with scale, mix and momentum", 12 Feb 2026.)*

**Competitive split — use the primary disclosures, not the trackers.** Blinkit and Instamart both disclose
quarterly. Zepto is private and every Zepto number in circulation is press-reported; it cannot sit in the
primary tier.

*Blinkit — Eternal Q1FY27 shareholders' letter, 22 July 2026 (PRIMARY), verbatim:*

> "Quick commerce (Blinkit) - NOV grew 86% YoY to INR 17,132 crore with QoQ growth at 19%. 200 net new
> stores were added, taking the total count to 2,443. Adjusted EBITDA improved for the fifth consecutive
> quarter to 0.6% of NOV, resulting in INR 102 crore profit (as compared to INR 162 crore loss last year)."

> "Today, we operate about 19 million sqft. of store and warehousing space across 300+ cities. We've
> invested ~INR 3,000 crore capex over the past four years to build this network, and as we continue to
> expand, the investments will continue."

*Instamart — Swiggy Q1FY27 shareholder letter, July 2026 (PRIMARY), verbatim:*

> "GOV grew 39.8% YoY to INR 7,907 Cr, NOV growth was at 38.9% to INR 5,817 Cr (sequential growth at 3%)"
> "Total darkstore area increased to more than 4.9 Mn sq ft (+14.6% YoY), net addition of 28 darkstores to
> reach 1,171 stores across 131 cities"
> "We have built out a very strong network across the 131 cities that we are currently operating in with
> store utilization at ~40% with significant head-room to grow of the current network. At the same time,
> the underlying volume growth in high growth neighborhoods necessitates new store opening and we expect to
> add ~75 stores in Q2FY27."

**[DERIVED] Blinkit vs Instamart.** On the same NOV definition and the same quarter (Apr–Jun 2026):
INR 17,132 cr vs INR 5,817 cr = **Blinkit is 2.95× Instamart**, and Blinkit runs **2,443 vs 1,171** dark
stores (2.09×). Growth is also diverging: **+86% YoY vs +38.9% YoY**. This is the defensible statement of
the competitive split. *Do not* state a three-way percentage share — see Open questions.

Instamart is the slower-growing of the two and says so, attributing it to a deliberate purge:

> "We were able to achieve that by weaning away unprofitable users (~4M+ over the last 3 quarters) and
> orders which were contributing negatively to the financial performance while ensuring that the retained
> users continued to get better experience on our platform."

### 2. Average order value and basket composition

**AOV.** Two sourced numbers, on different bases — keep them apart:

- **Instamart nAOV INR 508**, verbatim: "nAOV grew 12.1% YoY to INR 508 (from INR 453), led by sustained
  non-grocery selection mix and larger-basket buying behaviour across user cohorts". (Swiggy Q1FY27, PRIMARY.)
- **Industry ~INR 460**, verbatim: "AOV (post MRP discounts and excluding delivery fees) remained flat MoM,
  but still grew ~5% YoY to ~₹460". (Redseer, Jan 2026.)

**Blinkit does not disclose AOV directly**, but discloses a store-productivity figure that implies basket
scale, verbatim from the Q1FY27 letter's revised steady-state assumptions:

> "NOV per day, per store: INR 11 lakh (vs INR 7 lakh earlier), driven by larger store sizes and more
> efficient warehousing"

**Basket composition.** Bain is the only tiered source that splits the basket, verbatim:

> "Q-commerce plays dual roles across categories. It acts as a convenience channel for habit-forming
> household essentials (which account for 85%–90% of GMV) and as a fulfillment channel that delights
> customers in select discretionary categories."

And on pack size — directly relevant to impulse snacking, verbatim:

> "Q-commerce is also characterized by top-up missions, resulting in lower average order value and smaller
> pack sizes."

> "...top-up missions (high salience of small packs) requiring tailored, mission-specific assortment,
> pricing, and marketing strategies."

⚠️ **No source found that isolates "impulse / snacks / confectionery" as a share of the q-commerce basket.**
Bain's 85–90% "household essentials" bucket almost certainly contains packaged snacks but is not broken out.
This is the single biggest gap in the lane — see Open questions.

### 3. Channel split for snacking specifically

This is where platform disclosures stop being useful and **FMCG-maker commentary** takes over.

**The urban baseline (NIQ, PRIMARY).** From NIQ's "GST 2.0 Transition Reshapes India's FMCG Growth
Landscape", 13 March 2026, data period OND 2025 — verbatim fragments:

> "6% of Urban India FMCG sales"
> "14% across all metros"
> "18% in the top 8 metros"
> "Southern metros have surpassed 21% E-commerce share"
> "Quick commerce — contributing over three-fourths of E-commerce FMCG sales"
> "India's FMCG sector recorded value growth of 7.8% year-on-year"
> "Modern Trade recorded a threefold acceleration in OND 2025 compared to Q3 2025"
> "Rural regions recorded 2.9% volume growth" / "Urban markets grew 2.3%, supported by recovery in Metro
> consumption"

**[DERIVED]** Combining #2 and #3: in the **top 8 metros, quick commerce is roughly 13–14% of all FMCG
sales** (18% × ~0.75). For a persona living in Mumbai/Delhi/Bengaluru, that is the right order of magnitude
— roughly **one FMCG rupee in seven** now goes through a q-commerce app. Nationally it is far smaller
(6% urban e-commerce × 0.75 ≈ 4.5%).

⚠️ **State the assumption when using this.** NIQ's "over three-fourths" is q-commerce's share of
e-commerce FMCG measured across **urban India as a whole**, not a top-8-metro figure. Metros are
q-commerce's core market, so the metro ratio is plausibly *higher* than three-fourths — which makes
13–14% an approximation and most likely a **floor**, not a midpoint. Present it as "roughly one rupee in
seven, at least" rather than as a precise share.

**Biscuits — Britannia (company commentary).** Storyboard18, 12 May 2026, reporting the FY26 earnings call.
CEO **Rakshit Hargave** and Chief Commercial Officer **Vipin Kataria**:

- E-commerce is **6%** of domestic business, up from **4%** a year prior.
- Hargave, verbatim: **"If you apply that filter, you will actually realize that our e-commerce contribution
  is upwards of 12%, which is best in class"** — the "filter" being the exclusion of the low-unit-price
  packs that do not sell online at all.
- **Nearly 70%** of Britannia's online business comes from quick-commerce platforms, expected to rise to
  **85%** as Amazon and Flipkart expand rapid delivery.
- Adjacent categories (cakes, rusks) are growing online at **2.7×** the pace of the core business.
- **The mechanism that matters for snacking:** Britannia deliberately does **not** push its ₹5 and ₹10 packs
  online, preferring premium/indulgent SKUs. Traditional e-commerce skews to staples; quick commerce drives
  premium and indulgent demand.

This is the cleanest published statement of the structural point: **the ₹5/₹10 impulse pack is a kirana
object, and it does not travel to q-commerce.** The q-commerce snacking basket is a larger-pack, more
premium, more indulgent basket than the kirana snacking basket.

**Redseer on packaged F&B.** Report *"Reinventing Packaged F&B with Quick Commerce"*, 18 March 2026 —
the landing page yields only these verbatim lines (full report is gated):

> "In India's $100Bn+ packaged F&B market is projected to expand to ~$150 billion+ by 2030."
> "the quick commerce channel alone is expected to scale from $4 billion today to more than $25 billion in
> GMV by 2030."

The report's stated category focus is "ready-to-cook products, better-for-you and functional beverages, and
indulgent food like chocolates," across "Gen Z, Millennials, and Bharat households". **[DERIVED]** $4bn of
$100bn+ ≈ **~4% of packaged F&B currently through q-commerce nationally**, rising to ~17% by 2030 on
Redseer's own projection — consistent with the NIQ national arithmetic above.

**Savoury snacks / staples — ITC (company disclosure).** ITC groups its channels as "NewGen Channels",
defined in its own Q4 & FY26 results presentation (21 May 2026), verbatim:

> "NewGen Channels includes e-Commerce, Quick Commerce & Modern Trade"
> "Premium portfolio and New Gen Channels continue to perform well"
> "Robust growth in New Gen Channels"
> "Sharp execution of business plans | Collaborations | Format-based assortments | Category-specific
> sell-out strategies"

Note **"Format-based assortments"** — ITC is explicitly building different assortments per channel format,
which is the same mechanism Britannia describes.

The percentages were given on the FY26 call rather than in the deck; reported by Outlook Business
(20 July 2026), verbatim:

> "quick commerce sales grew 57% in FY26"
> "Digitally enabled sales, together with modern trade, contribute 34% of the company's FMCG portfolio"
> "ITC's products are available across nearly seven million retail outlets across the country"

⚠️ ITC's 34% bundles modern trade with digital, so it is **not** comparable to Britannia's 6% e-commerce
figure. Do not put them in the same table as if they measured the same thing.

⚠️ Category-level channel splits for **chocolate** and **savoury snacks** specifically were not found at
citable quality. Mondelez India and Parle are unlisted in India and do not publish channel mix. See Open
questions.

### 4. Kirana

**What is solid.** NIQ's channel figures are the reliable frame, and they are stated as e-commerce share,
so the traditional-trade share is the residual: e-commerce is **6% of urban India FMCG sales** and **18% in
the top 8 metros** (OND 2025). Everything not e-commerce is modern trade + general trade, and NIQ notes
Modern Trade "recorded a threefold acceleration in OND 2025 compared to Q3 2025" — i.e. **the channel taking
share from kirana in metros is not only q-commerce; modern trade re-accelerated too.**

**Is kirana's share actually declining?** In metros, yes, mechanically — 18% e-commerce share in the top 8
metros did not exist five years ago. But the national picture is much less dramatic (6% urban, and rural is
barely touched), and the FMCG majors have publicly re-committed to general trade: ITC, Nestlé, Coca-Cola,
Tata Consumer, Dabur, Reliance and Parle were reported in mid-2025 to be pivoting back to general trade
after a year dominated by q-commerce partnerships (Business Standard, 23 June 2025 — **fetch returned HTTP
403; headline and framing only, no number extracted; treat as unverified**).

**Counter-evidence: traditional trade GAINED share in one snacking category.** Kantar Worldpanel's
out-of-home study (23 July 2024, Worldpanel OOH Barometer + Brand Footprint) found, verbatim:

> "India (+64%) and the UK (+32%) are the chief catalysts for OOH growth in salty snacks"
> "traditional trade has seen the biggest uplift in spend within this category, from a 31% share of value
> in Q1 of 2023 to 35% in 2024"
> "India was the star performer, with OOH increasing its market share from 66% to 71% in the last year"
> "driven by a huge 44% rise in spend"
> "With penetration of 40%, Haldiram's leads India's savoury snack segment"

This is important and cuts against the simple "q-commerce is eating kirana" narrative: for
**out-of-home salty snacking**, traditional trade *grew* its value share from 31% to 35%. Out-of-home
consumption — the packet bought and eaten away from the house — is structurally a kirana/traditional-trade
occasion that a 10-minute home-delivery app does not serve. *(Caveat: this data is 2024, the oldest in this
lane. Treat as directional and flag for refresh.)*

**Impulse confectionery is historically a checkout-counter kirana business** — this is the received wisdom
and Britannia's ₹5/₹10 commentary is consistent with it, but **no citable source was found that quantifies
the checkout-counter/impulse share of kirana confectionery sales.** Kantar's finding that 75%+ of impulse-
category purchases are now pre-planned (Section 5) suggests the checkout-counter model was already eroding
*before* q-commerce scaled. See Open questions.

⚠️ **The "13 million kiranas / 90% of FMCG" figure is NOT used here.** It circulates widely (Invest India,
Kirana Club, trade press) but every instance traces back to another secondary page, not to a named
consultancy study or government dataset with a working URL. Per the sourcing rule, it goes in Open questions.

### 5. What changes about the DECISION in each channel

This is the best-evidenced part of the lane, and it comes from one figure in Bain HISO 2026. Verbatim:

> "Q-commerce is geared towards shopping missions that are led by predetermined needs with sub-five-minute
> session durations vs. more than ten minutes in e-retail and eight times higher visit-to-order conversion.
> This is reflected in a higher share of search-led GMV, fewer product pages viewed per order, and lower
> reliance on cash-on-delivery compared to traditional e-retail. Q-commerce is also characterized by top-up
> missions, resulting in lower average order value and smaller pack sizes."

> "Brands and sellers can capitalize on the nature of Q-commerce shopping missions by tailoring assortment
> at the micro-market level and optimizing marketing to secure first-screen visibility and capture
> high-intent moments."

**Read for persona construction — the three decisions are genuinely different:**

| | Kirana checkout counter | Q-commerce app | Traditional e-retail / monthly list |
|---|---|---|---|
| Session | Seconds, physical, at the till | **<5 min**, search-led | **>10 min**, browse-led |
| Intent | Unplanned, sight-triggered | **Predetermined need**, then top-up | Planned, replenishment |
| Conversion | n/a | **8× e-retail** | baseline |
| Discovery surface | Shelf/counter visibility, shopkeeper | **First screen + search results** | Search listings, ads, reviews |
| Pack | ₹5/₹10 single-serve | **Small packs, high salience**, but not ₹5/₹10 | Bulk / multipack |
| Payment | Cash / UPI | UPI, low COD | Mixed, COD declining |

The critical asymmetry for an ad-reaction simulation: **on q-commerce the shopper arrives with a need and
converts 8× more often, but browses far less** ("fewer product pages viewed per order"). Advertising's job
shifts from *creating* consideration in a long browse to *winning the first screen* in a five-minute,
search-led session. Bain says exactly this ("secure first-screen visibility and capture high-intent
moments").

**⭐ The single most important finding in this lane: impulse is no longer impulsive.** Kantar India,
9 July 2025, by **Sreyoshi Maitra, Executive Vice President, India, Insights Division, Kantar** —
verbatim:

> "over 75% of purchases in traditional impulse categories—chocolates, confectioneries, cookies, salty
> snacks, and fizzy drinks—are now planned before entering a store"

> "the moment of impulse has moved from the store to the screen—shaped by social media, targeted ads, and
> convenience-driven buying behaviours"

Read this carefully, because it reframes the whole lane. **The categories Kantar names are exactly the
snacking categories in this pack.** The claim is not that q-commerce made shoppers impulsive — it is that
the *decision has moved upstream, out of the store entirely*. By the time the shopper reaches any
channel — kirana counter, app, or supermarket aisle — three quarters of the choice is already made,
and it was made on a screen.

The implication for ad-reaction simulation is direct: **advertising for impulse snacks is no longer
competing for a two-second glance at a till. It is competing to be the thing already decided on before
the shopper opens the app.** Bain's "predetermined needs" framing and Kantar's "planned before entering a
store" are the same finding observed from two different ends of the funnel.

*Caveat to state plainly:* Kantar's article cites "shopper studies" without naming the panel, sample or
methodology, and gives no link to an underlying report. It is a named consultancy publishing under a named
senior author, so it clears the tiering bar, but the 75% figure is **not independently verifiable** and
should be attributed in the pack as "Kantar India" rather than presented as an industry constant.

⚠️ **Beyond Kantar, published behavioural research specifically on how q-commerce changes impulse buying in
India exists only in low-quality journals** (IJRISS, ResearchGate preprints, ABAcademies). These were found
but are **not cited here** — they are not tiered sources and several are in predatory or non-indexed
venues. See Open questions.

### 6. Path to purchase for impulse snacks

**No published Indian FMCG snacking journey study (trigger → consideration → purchase) was found at citable
quality.** What can be assembled from tiered sources is a partial skeleton:

- **Trigger:** happens **off-channel, on a screen**. Kantar: "over 75% of purchases in traditional impulse
  categories—chocolates, confectioneries, cookies, salty snacks, and fizzy drinks—are now planned before
  entering a store" and "the moment of impulse has moved from the store to the screen—shaped by social
  media, targeted ads, and convenience-driven buying behaviours". Bain independently characterises
  q-commerce demand as "predetermined needs" and "top-up missions" — i.e. the trigger is largely *pre-app*,
  not created by the app.
- **Consideration:** compressed. "sub-five-minute session durations", "fewer product pages viewed per
  order", "higher share of search-led GMV". (Bain HISO 2026.)
- **Purchase:** "eight times higher visit-to-order conversion" vs e-retail. (Bain HISO 2026.)
- **Frequency ramp after first purchase:** a NIQ-attributed figure circulates (2–3 orders/month in month one
  rising to 8–12 by month six) but **it was only found on a secondary blog, not on an NIQ page** — it is in
  Open questions, not here.

The general e-retail journey (not snacking-specific) is mapped in Bain HISO 2026 Figure 17 across
Discovery → Consideration → Purchase → Payment → Post-purchase, with these sourced deltas:

> "Hyper-personalization enables faster product discovery — ~1.2 pps Higher home page conversion from
> high-intent widgets (2025)"
> "Ads increasingly drive higher consideration — +20% Higher add-to-cart and purchases per impression YOY
> vs. organic listings (2025)"
> "Shoppers mature with growing vintage on e-retail — 2x+ TPC and categories per customer in 2025 (for new
> shoppers onboarded in 2020)"

### 7. Discovery — where urban Indians find new snack brands

**Retail media is now a first-order discovery surface.** Bain HISO 2026, citing Dentsu e4m, verbatim:

> "E-retail advertising accounts for 1 in 4 digital ad dollars"
> "~25% Share of e-retail ads spend in digital ads spend (2025 vs. ~18% in 2023)"
> "~2x Growth in e-retail ad spend vs. social media ad spend (2023–25)"

Growth drivers listed verbatim: "Higher purchase intent (vs. other digital ad media)", "Precise targeting
(based on user behavior, search patterns, and purchase history)", "Tailored solutions", "Measurable
outcomes". Bain maps ad formats across Awareness (display, brand store, video), Consideration (targeted
static banners, add-to-cart widget, gamified engagement, **influencer ads — "Live demos, short-form
explainer videos"**, sponsored product ads, retargeting) and Purchase (checkout/post-purchase ads).

**Ad-driven trial, sourced:** "+20% Higher add-to-cart and purchases per impression YOY vs. organic
listings (2025)" — this is the closest citable number to "does advertising drive trial on these surfaces",
and it is an e-retail figure, not q-commerce-specific.

**Social discovery.** The prior edition (Bain × Flipkart *How India Shops Online 2025*, 26 March 2025) is
the source usually quoted, verbatim:

> "70% of Gen Z shoppers discover brands online and make quicker purchase decisions, browsing fewer search
> listings than millennials before buying."

The 2026 edition lists "Social media (trend discovery via influencers)" and "Immersive experience (videos,
feeds)" as Gen Z discovery behaviours in its Gen Z figure, and notes "Two-thirds of chat and social media
users are yet to shop online" — i.e. social reach massively exceeds social-originated commerce.

**Instamart as a launchpad — company commentary.** Swiggy positions the platform explicitly as a discovery
and launch surface for brands, verbatim from the Q1FY27 letter:

> "we have partnered with 400+ brands to curate higher-quality, value-forward alternatives across core
> daily-use categories at compelling price points. We have successfully completed the first phase of this
> initiative by launching such 'Switch to Better' selection across 50 key product categories which now
> contribute to more than 15% of the category sales in a very short time."

> "Our objective for the coming quarters is to scale this overall differentiated proposition across more
> categories and occasions, partnering with brands that view Instamart as a premier launchpad."

Blinkit's equivalent is assortment/premiumisation rather than discovery language, verbatim:

> "This quarter, we continued to make progress on assortment expansion in the top eight cities and
> geographic expansion in the next 30. Going forward, premiumisation through launch of 'gourmet' stores in
> select locations in top eight cities will also contribute to assortment expansion on the platform. These
> gourmet stores offer our customers the ability to buy curated premium brands across categories."

**Word-of-mouth and social trial — Mondelēz (company primary).** *State of Snacking 2026*, published
17 June 2026, produced with **Mintel** and **Black Swan Data**. Two India-specific figures, verbatim:

> "In India, 79% find it fun to try new snacks with friends just to see their reactions"
> "In India, 78% of adults see snacking as a way to explore different cultures and cuisines"

For comparison, the report's nearest social-discovery figure is from another market — verbatim: "In
Brazil, 31% of snack consumers try snacks they discover on social media", "rising to 36% among those under
34". **Mondelēz did not publish the equivalent social-discovery percentage for India**, so the Brazil
figure must not be transplanted.

The Indian numbers support a *social-trial* mechanism (new snacks are tried with other people, for their
reaction) rather than a solo-scroll-to-cart mechanism. Combined with Kantar's "the moment of impulse has
moved from the store to the screen", the defensible picture is: **screen creates the intent, other people
validate the trial, the channel merely fulfils.**

⚠️ **No sourced number was found for TV vs Instagram vs in-store as the discovery source for a new snack
brand in India**, and none for q-commerce app tiles/search as a discovery surface expressed as a share.
See Open questions.

### 8. Repeat and lapse

**No published trial-to-repeat data for Indian D2C / better-for-you snack brands was found.** This appears
genuinely not to exist at citable quality. What does exist is **platform-level** retention, which is a
different construct (retention on the app, not repeat of a brand) but is the best available anchor and is
fully primary.

*Blinkit — Eternal Q1FY27 letter, verbatim:*

> "Q4 retention (% of customers placing at least 1 order in the 4th quarter after acquisition): average
> across all cohorts is 46%, with every successive cohort improving - the most recent cohort (Q1FY26) is
> at 50%"
> "Retention improves with time - Q8 retention at 48%, Q12 at 49% - most recent cohorts that have completed
> Q8 and Q12 are even higher at 52% each"
> "NOV retention compounds even faster - Q4 at 150%, Q8 at 215%, Q12 at 279% average retention for all
> cohorts. In other words, a cohort of customers is spending nearly 3x with us three years after
> acquisition than they did in their first quarter"

*Instamart — Swiggy Q1FY27 letter, verbatim:*

> "We enter Q2FY27 with our most resilient, high-frequency, and highly retained transacting user base of
> the past year with 1M retention of the transacting user base increasing to 61% (from 55% in Q1FY26)."

**One SKU-level repeat number exists and it is the most useful single figure in this section**, because it
is about a *product choice*, not app retention. Swiggy Q1FY27 letter, verbatim:

> "Customers who have purchased 'Switch' SKUs have exhibited 30% higher retention rate compared to
> benchmark cohorts."

I.e. a better-for-you / curated SKU purchase is associated with **30% higher retention than benchmark
cohorts** on the same platform. Note this is an association reported by an interested party, not a
controlled result.

## Open questions

These could not be sourced to a working URL with a verbatim sentence. **They are deliberately left empty
rather than estimated.**

1. **Impulse / snacks / confectionery as a share of the q-commerce basket.** Bain gives "household
   essentials 85–90% of GMV" but no snacking cut. No platform discloses category mix. *This is the number
   the pack most wants and it does not appear to be public.*
2. **Chocolate and savoury-snack channel splits specifically** (q-commerce vs kirana vs modern trade vs
   traditional e-com). Britannia's biscuit commentary is the only company-level datapoint obtained;
   Mondelez India and Parle are unlisted and do not publish, and no Nestlé India q-commerce percentage was
   found in the FY26 disclosures searched.
3. **Number of kirana stores in India.** The "13 million" figure is everywhere and pinned nowhere. Needs a
   named origin (NIQ retail census, government, Bain) before use.
4. **Kirana share of FMCG as a single number.** "90%" and "85%" both circulate without checkable origin.
   NIQ's e-commerce shares (6% urban / 18% top-8 metros) are the sourced way to talk about this; the kirana
   residual should not be stated as a hard figure because it conflates modern trade with general trade.
5. **Checkout-counter / impulse share of kirana confectionery sales.** Received wisdom, no source found.
6. **Three-way q-commerce market share (Blinkit / Zepto / Instamart) as percentages.** Figures such as
   "Blinkit 48% / Instamart 24% / Zepto 22%" circulate attributed to Datum Intelligence via Reuters, but I
   could not open a Reuters or Datum page carrying them, and the trackers blend GMV share with order-volume
   share. **Use the [DERIVED] Blinkit:Instamart NOV ratio (2.95:1) instead**, which rests on two primary
   filings. Zepto, being private, has no primary disclosure at all.
7. **Methodology behind Kantar's 75% figure.** Kantar India's "over 75% of purchases in traditional impulse
   categories… are now planned before entering a store" is the load-bearing claim of this lane, but the
   article names no panel, sample size, period or underlying report. **Worth commissioning a direct ask to
   Kantar before a brand's research team asks us.** Beyond Kantar, the only other published behavioural work
   on q-commerce and impulse buying in India is in non-indexed or predatory venues (IJRISS, ABAcademies,
   ResearchGate preprints) and is not used.
8. **A full Indian FMCG snacking path-to-purchase journey study** (trigger → consideration → purchase) with
   stage-by-stage numbers. Kantar and NielsenIQ almost certainly hold this as syndicated commercial work;
   nothing public was found. Section 6 assembles a skeleton from three separate sources rather than one study.
9. **Discovery source split for new snack brands** (Instagram/reels vs q-commerce tiles/search vs in-store
   vs TV), as percentages. Not found. The Bain 2025 "70% of Gen Z discover brands online" figure is the
   closest and it is neither snacking-specific nor a channel split. Mondelēz publishes a social-discovery
   percentage for **Brazil** (31%) but **not for India** — do not transplant it.
9b. **Healthy-snack GMV on q-commerce.** A figure of "₹327.7 crore in Q1 2026, more than doubled" appears
   on Indian Retailer, but the page returns HTTP 403 and the underlying data source could not be
   identified. Not used.
10. **Trial-to-repeat conversion for Indian D2C / better-for-you snack brands.** Not found. Platform
    retention (findings 21–23) is a different construct and should not be presented as brand repeat rate.
11. **NIQ post-first-purchase frequency ramp** ("2–3 orders/month in month one → 8–12 by month six").
    Attributed to NIQ on a secondary blog; not found on any NIQ page. Do not use until pinned.
12. **Blinkit AOV.** Eternal does not disclose it; only NOV per day per store (INR 11 lakh steady-state
    assumption). The Instamart INR 508 and Redseer ~INR 460 figures should not be read as Blinkit's.

## Notes for the pack author

0. **⭐ Start here: the impulse snack decision is made before the channel is entered.** Kantar India puts
   **75%+** of chocolate / confectionery / cookie / salty-snack / fizzy-drink purchases as planned before
   entering a store, with "the moment of impulse… moved from the store to the screen". Bain says the same
   thing from the platform side ("predetermined needs", sub-5-minute sessions, 8× conversion). A persona
   who "sees a bar at the till and grabs it" is modelling a 2015 shopper. The 2026 shopper decided on
   Instagram on Tuesday and collected it on Blinkit on Thursday.
1. **The metro figure, not the national figure, is the one a persona lives in.** Nationally e-commerce is
   6% of urban FMCG; in the top 8 metros it is 18%, and roughly 13–14% of FMCG goes through q-commerce
   there **[DERIVED]**. A Bengaluru or Mumbai persona should treat the app as a normal weekly channel; a
   Tier-2 persona should not.
2. **The ₹5/₹10 pack does not exist online.** Britannia says so explicitly. Any persona reasoning about
   impulse snacking at a single-serve price point is reasoning about a *kirana counter*, not an app. The
   q-commerce snack basket is larger-pack, more premium, more indulgent.
3. **Q-commerce is not a browsing surface.** Sub-5-minute sessions, search-led, fewer pages per order, 8×
   conversion. A persona does not "discover" a snack brand by scrolling Blinkit the way they scroll
   Instagram — they arrive with a need and the first screen decides. Ad reactions should reflect that
   compression.
4. **Kirana is not simply losing.** Kantar found traditional trade *gaining* value share in out-of-home
   salty snacks (31% → 35%). The occasion splits the channel: **at-home stock-up migrates to the app;
   out-of-home, eaten-on-the-move snacking stays with the shop on the corner.** A persona should use both,
   for different occasions, not treat one as replacing the other.
5. **Do not let a persona quote a snacking channel-split percentage.** It is not public (Open question 2).
   Personas can describe *behaviour* ("I get my chips off Zepto now, but the ₹10 packs I still pick up
   downstairs") without asserting a market statistic.
6. **Two numbers that will get checked hardest**, because they are the most quotable and the least solid:
   the Kantar 75% (no published methodology — Open question 7) and any three-way Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart
   share split (not derivable from primary filings — Open question 6). Handle both as flagged above.
