## Detail

### 1. Household penetration and purchase frequency

**Biscuits — the only category with a sourceable penetration figure.**

Britannia Industries Annual Report 2020-21, Management Discussion & Analysis, verbatim:

> "Biscuit is the largest category in the food business in India. It is present in the consumption basket of virtually every Indian Family as an essential product. It is one of the most deeply penetrated categories in the country, reaching over 90% of the households. However, the per capita consumption of biscuits in India is relatively low at 2 kgs versus 10 kgs in certain developed countries. The low per capita consumption and high levels of penetration continue to provide excellent opportunities to increase consumption through proactive interventions and strategies."

And in the same report's outlook section:

> "The biscuit segment in India continues to provide significant opportunities for growth as the per capita consumption is much lower than that of developed countries. High levels of household penetration (above 90%) augurs well for increase in consumption."

**Negative finding — the current annual report dropped the numbers.** We read the full Britannia Annual Report FY2024-25 (170pp, https://media.britannia.co.in/B_Il_Annual_Report_for_FY_2024_25_b6d95d1717.pdf). It contains **no quantified household-penetration figure and no per-capita consumption figure** for any category. Its biscuit commentary is entirely qualitative. The FY2020-21 report above is therefore the most recent *primary* source for these two numbers, and it is five years old.

**Savoury snacks — brand-level only.** Kantar, 23 July 2024, verbatim:

> "With penetration of 40%, Haldiram's leads India's savoury snack segment through continually finding new space in which to innovate and gain new buyers – one of the key drivers identified in Kantar's Blueprint for Brand Growth."

That is a *brand* penetration figure, not a category one. No category-level penetration for savoury snacks or chocolate could be sourced.

**Purchase frequency — published only in composite form.** Kantar/Worldpanel's public India output is Consumer Reach Points, which *combine* penetration and frequency and therefore cannot be decomposed. Kantar defines CRP verbatim as:

> "a measure that combines data on population, penetration, and frequency to quantify consumers' choices"

*Brand Footprint India 2026* (released 13 August 2026, i.e. the day before this compilation) reports, per secondhand trade coverage: Parle 8,515mn in-home CRPs, Britannia 8,296mn, Amul 6,922mn; Britannia leads out-of-home with 619mn CRPs, then Balaji 499mn, Haldiram's 476mn, Cadbury 450mn; "FMCG brand choices in India grew 5.1% in 2025." Balaji rose 11th→9th with 2,876mn and Haldiram's completed the in-home top 10 at 2,780mn. **We could not open the Worldpanel primary release — these figures are secondhand and tiered accordingly.**

**Panel basis.** Worldpanel by Numerator, India coverage page: **81,200 Indian homes**, **97 categories** across Food & Beverages, Personal Care and Home Care, tracked monthly. (https://market.worldpanelbynumerator.com/global/Coverage/worldpanel/India)

**Trips and occasions.** Kantar, 14 March 2024, verbatim:

> "The further relaxation of COVID restrictions in 2023 led to Indian consumers making an average of 14 extra trips compared to the previous year. The number of out-of-home (OOH) occasions stood at 43; a surge of nearly 50% over the year."

> "Increased trips resulted in a notable uptick in pack purchases, with a staggering 81 more packs bought on an average – twice as many as in 2022."

⚠ The article does **not** state the base or period for "43" (per household per year is the likely reading, but it is not stated). Do not build on this number without confirming the base.

Same article, on which categories drove growth, verbatim:

> "Biscuits grew most substantially, followed closely by salty snacks, ice cream, and chocolates. This shift highlights consumers' inclination towards indulgent and convenient snacking options."

### 3. Snacking frequency

**Mondelez/Mintel, 2026 State of Snacking, p.12** — verbatim, in full, with the cross-country lines for context:

> "66% of U.S. adults snack at least once a day / 26% of U.S. adults snack twice a day, 23% snack once a day and 17% snack three times a day or more"
> "61% of Germans who snack eat snacks at least daily / 33% of German adults who snack typically snack twice a day or more and 28% snack once a day"
> "**41% of Indian adults snack once a day or more / 25% of Indian adults typically snack once a day, 12% have snacked twice a day and 4% ate snacks three times a day or more in the last month**"
> "70% of Australians who snack have one daily / 30% of Australian snack consumers typically snack once a day, 28% snack twice a day and 13% snack three times a day or more between meals"

Base line, verbatim: *"U.S.: 2,000 internet users aged 18+; Germany: 1,981 internet users aged 16+ who have eaten snacks in the last two weeks; India: 3,000 Indian adults aged 18+; Australia: 988 internet users aged 18+ who have eaten snacks in the last two weeks"*. Source line: *"Mintel, November 2025, August 2025, January 2025"* (three dates for four markets; the India mapping is not stated).

> **⚠ DO NOT read India's 41% as "Indians snack less than Americans."** The bases are not comparable. India's base is **3,000 Indian adults 18+** — i.e. *all adults*, including non-snackers. Germany's and Australia's bases are **people who have eaten snacks in the last two weeks**. A denominator that includes non-snackers mechanically produces a lower rate. The report's own methodology (p.8) also states that India Reports use **"Offline research (i.e., face-to-face interviews) with adults aged 18+"** via **Ipsos Observer**, while most other markets are online panels via Kantar Profiles — a mode difference that independently moves self-reported frequency.

**A conflicting figure we are NOT using.** Mintel's free article *"Exploring India's snacking culture"* (https://www.mintel.com/insights/food-and-drink/exploring-indias-snacking-culture/) opens: *"Majority of Indians snack at least twice a day–many of whom view snacks as an indulgent treat."* This **contradicts** the same firm's flagship 2026 figure (12% twice a day). The article carries no visible publication date. **Excluded** — undated and irreconcilable.

### 4. Meal replacement

**Mondelez/Mintel, 2026 State of Snacking, p.40**, headed *"CONSUMERS ACROSS MARKETS ARE CHOOSING SNACKS TO REPLACE MEALS"* — verbatim:

> "**22% ... of Indian snack consumers eat snacks to replace breakfast, lunch or dinner**"
> "44% ... of Australian snack consumers agree they snack instead of having a proper meal at least once a week"
> "69% ... of U.S. consumers who buy snack, nutrition or performance bars agree a bar can be a good replacement for a meal, such as lunch"

Base, verbatim: *"India: 2,632 Indian adults aged 18+ who have consumed snacks in the month to December 2025; Australia: 988 internet users aged 18+ who have eaten snacks in the last two weeks; US: 1,652 internet users aged 18 who purchase bars"*. Source: *"Mintel, December 2025, January 2025, November 2025"*.

Note this India base **is** snack consumers (2,632), unlike the frequency figure — so 22% is "of Indian snack consumers", not of all adults. The Australian 44% is a *frequency-qualified* measure ("at least once a week") and the Indian 22% is not, so they are not directly comparable either.

Report-level context, exec summary p.5, verbatim: *"Around half of consumers in select global markets occasionally replace meals with snacks"* and *"U.S. consumers have become more comfortable replacing meals with snacks from 2019-2025."*

**Supporting qualitative, primary.** Britannia Annual Report FY2024-25, verbatim:

> "Urbanisation, rapid growth of Quick Commerce and the shift towards nuclear family structures are further enhancing the role of biscuits as an accessible meal option."

### 5. Time-of-day distribution

**This is the question where the published record is most contradictory. Read all three blocks below before using any of it.**

**(a) Mondelez/Mintel 2026, p.18 — "Occasions When Consumers Do Not Eat Snacks, 2025".** Slide headline verbatim: *"THE EVENING SNACKING OCCASION IS RIPE FOR EXPANSION"*; sub-headline verbatim:

> "Afternoon and morning are the snacking occasions least likely to be skipped, especially for consumers in Brazil, Mexico, U.S., India, Indonesia, Philippines and Vietnam"

India's bars (read off the chart, verified against a rendered image of the page):

| | Don't eat morning snacks | Don't eat afternoon snacks | Don't eat evening snacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | **3%** | **4%** | **5%** |
| US | 23% | 22% | 23% |
| France | 43% | 39% | 58% |

Base, verbatim: *"Brazil, France, Germany and UK: 1,000 internet users aged 16+ in each market; Canada, Mexico, US, Australia, China, India, Indonesia, Philippines and Vietnam: 1,000 internet users aged 18+ in each market"*. Source: *"Mintel, March 2025 (morning, afternoon, evening)"*.

Read plainly: essentially **all Indian internet users eat snacks at all three dayparts** — only 3–5% skip any given one. India is the least "skipping" market in the study alongside Indonesia.

⚠ **This is a different base from the 41% figure** (1,000 internet users vs 3,000 offline all-adults) and the two cannot both be describing the same population. An urban-internet Indian appears to snack across the whole day; the all-adult offline India sample reports much lower daily frequency. For an *urban* persona pack, the internet-user base is the more relevant one — but the tension is unresolved in the published record.

**(b) Mondelez/Mintel 2026, p.31 — "Snacking Occasions When Consumers Seek an Indulgent Snack or Something That Feels Like a Treat, 2025".** Headline verbatim: *"CONSUMERS REACH FOR INDULGENT SNACKS IN THE EVENING AND AFTERNOON"*. India's values (read off the chart, verified against a rendered image):

| | Morning snack | Afternoon snack | Evening snack |
|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | **15%** | **19%** | **20%** |

Same base and source as (a). Indulgence skews later in the day, mildly.

**(c) Ganpule et al., *J Nutr* 2023, Table 2 — "Time" (India, two districts).** This is the only India daypart data from a peer-reviewed, face-to-face study. Percentages exactly as printed, by column:

| Time | Vizag rural | Vizag urban | Sonipat rural | Sonipat urban |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Morning | 85 (4.5) | 394 (**24.2**) | 672 (60.8) | 909 (**85.2**) |
| Afternoon | 352 (18.7) | 218 (**13.4**) | 667 (60.4) | 242 (**22.7**) |
| Evening | 1797 (95.3) | 1572 (**96.7**) | 303 (27.4) | 339 (**31.8**) |
| Late-night | 286 (15.2) | 216 (**13.3**) | 45 (4.1) | 9 (0.8) |
| Midnight | 1 (0.1) | (0.0) | (0.0) | (0.0) |

> **This is the most consequential single table in the lane.** In **urban Vizag** (Andhra Pradesh, south) snacking is an **evening** behaviour: 96.7% evening vs 13.4% afternoon. In **urban Sonipat** (Haryana, north) it is a **morning** behaviour: 85.2% morning vs 31.8% evening. **The two Indian cities are close to inverted.** There is no single "Indian daypart curve" to give a persona; the curve is regional.

⚠ Caveats that travel with (c) everywhere: adults **aged ≥30**, mean age **52.4 years**; only **two districts**; **56.1% rural** overall; snacking-behaviour fieldwork **August 2020 – February 2021** (COVID period); multi-select responses, so columns do not sum to 100%.

### 6. In-home vs out-of-home

**Purchase/value share — Kantar Worldpanel OOH Barometer.** India is a global outlier. Verbatim, Kantar 23 July 2024 (Q1 2024 data):

> "The proportion of value contributed by out-of-home (OOH) sales to the global snacks and non-alcoholic drinks market continues to escalate – with a share of 38% in Q1 of 2024, up from 36% in the same quarter in 2023 and 34% in 2022."

> "Growth in OOH snacks and drinks spend reached 14% in Q1 of this year, according to Kantar's Worldpanel latest OOH Barometer study. This represents significantly faster growth than in-home (3%), emphasising how essential OOH sales are to the strength of the sector as a whole. **India was the star performer, with OOH increasing its market share from 66% to 71% in the last year, driven by a huge 44% rise in spend.**"

> "**India (+64%) and the UK (+32%) are the chief catalysts for OOH growth in salty snacks. In India, snacking habits developed during the pandemic have become entrenched as a new part of everyday life.**"

> "The traditional trade channel rules supreme in India, where the success of snacks has transformed the FMCG market. This year there are six snack brands in the Brand Footprint Top 20 ranking, while those that have made the biggest jumps up the ranking also come from this sector, led by salty snack brand Bingo."

Verbatim, Kantar (Q2 2024 data):

> "The proportion of total 'in and out' value coming from OOH has risen for the 8th consecutive quarter, rising to 38% from 37% in Q2 of 2023. **Growth was fastest in India, with an uplift from 68% to 71%.**"

> "India saw the biggest surge in spend at 27%, followed by Brazil and Mexico."

> "Biscuits and salty snacks are once again the top-performers in OOH, with a 12% and 9% boost in value compared with last year. Both are growing at a faster rate than the overall OOH market – but their gains are not nibbling away at in-home sales, which have maintained a strong 3% growth in both categories."

So: **India ≈ 71% OOH value share vs 38% globally.** Note the two articles give different prior-year baselines (66% in Q1, 68% in Q2) because they are different quarters — cite the quarter.

**But "out-of-home" here means the purchase/immediate-consumption channel, NOT where the food is eaten.** The *J Nutr* study separates the two and the answer flips:

| | Vizag rural | Vizag urban | Sonipat rural | Sonipat urban |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Place preferred to eat** — Home | 1666 (88.3) | 1269 (**78.0**) | 948 (85.8) | 1006 (**94.3**) |
| Work place | 52 (2.8) | 302 (18.6) | 119 (10.8) | 49 (4.6) |
| Out of home | 168 (8.9) | 55 (**3.4**) | 38 (3.4) | 12 (**1.1**) |
| **Source** — Home | 1697 (90.0) | 495 (30.4) | 91 (8.2) | 156 (14.6) |
| Street vendor | 1050 (55.7) | 1110 (**68.3**) | 909 (82.3) | 900 (**84.3**) |
| Restaurant | 13 (0.7) | 75 (4.6) | 169 (15.3) | 158 (14.8) |

Study abstract, verbatim: *"Participants preferred to purchase out-of-home prepared snacks and eat them at home (86.6%) while watching television (69.4%) or with family/friends (49.3%)."*

> **Persona-relevant synthesis:** the Indian snack is **bought outside and eaten inside**. Sourcing is overwhelmingly street vendor / traditional trade (68–84% in urban cells); consumption location is overwhelmingly home (78–94% in urban cells). A persona who buys a namkeen packet from a kirana on the way home and eats it in front of the TV is consistent with *both* the Kantar OOH value data and the *J Nutr* location data. A persona who eats standing on the street is not well supported.

### 7. Sweet vs savoury

*J Nutr* 2023, results section, verbatim:

> "Savory snacks were the most frequently consumed snacks at all places. They were consumed everyday by around one-fourth of the participants from Sonipat; every week 3–5 times by ~30% participants. In Vizag, <10% of participants consumed savory snacks daily, whereas 60% of participants consumed them weekly. Of the savory snacks, the frequency of packaged salty snack consumption was higher than that of fried (results are not shown in the figure). Overall, the frequency of savory snack consumption was higher among urban participants than rural. **The frequency of consumption of sweet snacks was relatively low; only 10% consumed them weekly, whereas ~90% of participants never or rarely consumed them.**"

Abstract, verbatim: *"Savory snacks were the most preferred; 50% of the participants consumed them 3–5 times/wk."*

Discussion, verbatim: *"Most of the participants consumed savory snacks, while sweet snacks (including fruits) were consumed less frequently."*

Also from the same table — **cost per serving**, useful for price realism: savoury ₹13.1 (IQR 7.1–24.0); fruits ₹11.7 (IQR 8.3–16.7); sweet snacks bought outside the home ₹20.1 (IQR 10.2–20.3); beverages ₹7.5–8.3. Savoury in Vizag ₹14.3 vs Sonipat ₹10.1. **(2020-21 prices — inflate before use, and note this is street-vendor/unpackaged pricing, not MRP.)**

⚠ **This is frequency of consumption, NOT share of occasions.** It says savoury dominates *how often people eat each type*; it does not say what fraction of snacking *occasions* are sweet.

### 8. Solo vs shared / social snacking

**No published solo-vs-shared partition for India exists.** What does exist:

Mondelez/Mintel 2026, p.21, verbatim: *"**79% ... of Indian adults agree it is fun to try new snacks with friends to see their reactions**"* (base: *"India: 3,000 internet users aged 18+"*, Mintel Jan 2025). Exec summary adds: *"More than two-thirds of consumers from select markets say snacks are necessary when they are spending time socializing with friends or family."*

Mondelez/Mintel 2026, p.58, verbatim: *"**83% of Indian adults agree it is important to choose snacks that can create a joyful atmosphere for everyone**"* (base: *"India: 3,000 Indian adults aged 18+"*).

*J Nutr* 2023, Table 2, "Activities performed" — **with family/friends**: Vizag rural 66.2%, Vizag urban **87.4%**, Sonipat rural 23.0%, Sonipat urban **20.5%**. Watching TV: Vizag urban **85.4%**, Sonipat urban **70.7%**.

⚠ **These are not a solo/shared split.** "Activities performed" was a **rank-3 multi-select** ("The participants were asked to select 3 options of all and rank them in the order of preference"), so the categories overlap and do not partition occasions. And once again the two cities diverge wildly (87.4% vs 20.5%) — there is no national figure.

### 10. MoSPI Household Consumption Expenditure Survey — processed food and beverages

**MoSPI, HCES 2023-24 Fact Sheet, Statement 4** ("Absolute and percentage break-up of MPCE by item groups in 2023-24: All-India"), transcribed exactly:

| Item group | Rural MPCE (Rs.) | Urban MPCE (Rs.) | Rural % | **Urban %** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cereals & cereal substitutes | 206 | 263 | 4.99 | 3.76 |
| milk & milk products | 348 | 503 | 8.44 | 7.19 |
| **beverages, refreshments, processed food#** | **406** | **776** | **9.84** | **11.09** |
| **food total** | **1,939** | **2,776** | **47.04** | **39.68** |
| non-food total | 2,183 | 4,220 | 52.96 | 60.32 |
| **all items** | **4,122** | **6,996** | **100.00** | **100.00** |

Footnote verbatim: *"#includes purchased cooked meals"* — **critical**: this line item is **not** packaged snacks. It bundles restaurant and purchased cooked meals with beverages and processed food. Do not present it as a packaged-snacking number.

**Trend, urban India (Statement 6), verbatim row:** `beverages, processed food# etc.` — **8.98** (2011-12) → **10.64** (2022-23) → **11.09** (2023-24). Food total urban: 42.62 → 39.17 → 39.68.

**Derived (our arithmetic, not MoSPI's):** 776 ÷ 2,776 = **~28.0% of urban food spend** goes to beverages, refreshments and processed food — the **largest single food line item in the urban basket**, larger than milk (18.1%) or cereals (9.5%). MoSPI's own press note says this qualitatively, verbatim:

> "Beverages, refreshments and processed food continues to have the major expenditure share in 2023-24 in the food items basket of the rural and urban households."

**Headline MPCE, verbatim:** *"Average estimated MPCE in 2023-24 is observed to be Rs. 4,122 in rural India and Rs. 6,996 in urban India."* Urban non-food share ≈ 60%.

## What is NOT answerable from published data

This section is a deliberate deliverable. Each item below was searched for and **not found**; none should be filled in by inference.

1. **Occasion sizes — the core question — are not published for India, by anyone, in open sources.** There is no source that says "X% of Indian snacking occasions are hunger-bridge, Y% are treat, Z% are boredom." The occasion-level data that would answer this sits in Kantar/Worldpanel's commercial occasion-based usage panel and in paywalled Mintel/Euromonitor India reports. **Any occasion split in this pack must be labelled as a modelling assumption, not a finding.**

2. **There is no nationally representative Indian daypart distribution.** We have two datasets and they describe different things:
   - Mintel (n=1,000 **internet users**, Mar 2025) implies near-universal snacking at all three dayparts (only 3–5% skip any).
   - *J Nutr* (n=8,762, adults **≥30**, two districts, 2020-21) shows urban Vizag at 96.7% evening / 13.4% afternoon and urban Sonipat at 85.2% morning / 31.8% evening — **nearly inverted between two Indian cities**.
   These cannot be reconciled into one curve, and the *J Nutr* result is positive evidence that **a single national curve would be wrong**. Daypart should be treated as a **regional** persona variable, not a national constant.

3. **Snacking frequency itself is contested within a single research house.** Mintel's flagship 2026 report says 41% of Indian adults snack once a day or more (12% twice a day); Mintel's own undated web article says a "majority" snack at least twice a day. Base and mode differences (3,000 offline all-adults via Ipsos vs online panels) explain part of it, but the published record does not resolve it.

4. **Sweet vs savoury share of *occasions* is unpublished.** We have consumption *frequency* (savoury dominant; ~90% eat sweet snacks never or rarely) from one non-representative study. That is not an occasion share, and the *J Nutr* definition of "sweet snacks" (rasgulla, laddoo) is Indian mithai, **not chocolate or confectionery** — so it does not describe the chocolate category at all.

5. **Solo vs shared has never been published as a partition for India.** The available numbers ("with family/friends": 87.4% urban Vizag, 20.5% urban Sonipat) come from a rank-3 multi-select that does not partition occasions, and diverge by a factor of four between two cities.

6. **No current primary per-capita consumption figure exists for chocolate or savoury snacks in India.** Chocolate figures in circulation (20g / 50g / 120g / 150–165g / "<200g") are aggregator and essay content, mutually inconsistent, none traceable to Mondelez India or Nestlé India. The Bikaji commissioned industry report contains no per-capita savoury figure. The only usable per-capita number in this lane is Britannia's biscuit figure — and it is from FY2020-21.

7. **Category-level household penetration is published only for biscuits.** Chocolate and savoury-snack penetration exist only as *brand* figures (e.g. Haldiram's 40%). Kantar's public India output is CRPs, which fuse penetration and frequency and cannot be decomposed from the press material.

8. **Britannia's current annual report has removed the quantified market-structure claims** its FY2020-21 edition carried. This is a genuine loss of a primary source, not a search failure — we read the full FY2024-25 report.

9. **Weekday vs weekend snacking**: no India data found, in any source, at any tier.

10. **In-home vs out-of-home is answerable but ambiguous, and the two readings point opposite ways.** Kantar's 71% "OOH" is a *value/channel* share for snacks + non-alcoholic drinks; *J Nutr*'s 78–94% "home" is a *place-of-consumption* share. Both are correct. Any persona statement must specify which is meant. There is **no** published Indian split of *occasions* by consumption location on a national basis.

11. **"43 out-of-home occasions" (Kantar, 2023) has no stated base or period** in the source. Unusable as-is.

## Notes for whoever builds personas from this

- **Two numbers do the most work and are the most robust:** biscuits reach **>90% of Indian households** (Britannia, primary), and **~71% of India's snacks-and-drinks value is out-of-home** channel (Kantar, primary). Both are safe to state to a brand team.
- **Bought outside, eaten at home** is the single most defensible behavioural frame, and it is supported by two independent primary sources measuring different things.
- **Do not give every Indian persona the same daypart.** The evidence positively contradicts a national curve. Make daypart regional.
- **Do not quote India's 41% daily-snacking figure next to the US 66%** without the base caveat — a brand research team will catch it, and the comparison is invalid.
- **The HCES 11.09% line is not a snacking number.** It includes purchased cooked meals. Use it for share-of-wallet context only.
- **Anything resembling an occasion-size split in this pack is an assumption, not a finding.** Label it as such.
